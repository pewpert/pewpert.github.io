04 Vanilla JS · Firebase · No-framework

just say when.

The fastest way to pick a time everyone can make — solo-built, zero dependencies, free to run, and actually shipped.

The What

A group scheduling app. Each person marks when they're free — either in Basic mode (Morning / Afternoon / Evening per day, one tap) or Advanced mode (drag-paint 30-minute slots). The app surfaces the slots where everyone overlaps and highlights the match with a green banner: "You all found a time."

Built with plain HTML, CSS, and vanilla JavaScript — no framework, no build step. Firebase handles auth (Google + anonymous guest sign-in), real-time sync via Firestore onSnapshot, and hosting. Total payload is ~280 KB. Cost to run: $0 on Firebase's free tier, comfortably supporting 300–500 active users.

Live at justsaywhen-f70e5.web.app — currently being shared with friends.

The Why

Coordinating a meetup with three or more people in a group chat is a multi-hour ordeal. Someone suggests a time, someone can't make it, alternatives get proposed, people lose track, the question gets re-asked the following week. Existing tools like Doodle and When2Meet solve the problem but feel dated, are clunky on phones, and ask everyone to create an account they don't want.

The design goal was friction-to-zero: share a 6-character code or a link, tap your free slots, see the overlap. No account required to respond. No installation. Nothing to remember next time. One job, done.

How It Was Built

Two to three weeks of evenings, iterative. The deliberate choice to skip frameworks was partly a constraint — to genuinely understand what I was writing — and partly a product decision. No build step means anyone can open DevTools and read the source directly. Loads in under one second on cable.

The single hardest engineering problem was the mobile interaction model. The first version used drag-to-paint across availability cells. It felt fast — until it silently corrupted data. iOS Safari fires synthetic mousedown events roughly 300ms after touchend, which was toggling cells back off after users had toggled them on. The fix required three iterations: the shipped version uses a tap-arming pattern with a movement threshold and a 500ms synthetic-event guard to suppress the Safari compatibility events entirely.

A timezone bug caught in QA was the other near-miss. The day-range iterator was using toISOString().slice(0,10) — which silently shifts dates back one day for any user east of UTC. Every meetup in Asia-Pacific would have shown the wrong day. Replaced with a local-date helper before launch.

What I Learned

  • Shipping to real users surfaces bugs no test suite catches. Three rounds of self-directed QA found roughly 20 bugs ranging from a P0 timezone day-shift that would have broken every Asian user, to a P0 mobile data-corruption issue, to P3 UI polish. The bugs that matter most are the silent ones — the ones where users think the app worked and it didn't.

  • Anonymous auth removes the biggest source of drop-off. Every scheduling app loses users at the "create an account" step. Firebase Anonymous Auth lets any invitee tap "Continue as guest", enter a name, and immediately respond — no email, no password, no promise to remember a login. They can promote to a Google account later if they want their data across devices. Removing that barrier was a product decision that mattered more than any feature.

  • Platform inconsistencies are bugs you didn't write. The iOS Safari synthetic-event issue wasn't in my code. Neither was the scroll-position loss during Firestore snapshot re-renders, which required a requestAnimationFrame two-phase restore with a forced layout recalculation. Mobile browsers have a long memory of their own opinions about how touch and scroll should work. Testing on real devices, not just DevTools emulation, is non-negotiable.

  • No-framework is a valid choice — but you inherit the work. Vanilla JS meant I wrote my own state management, my own DOM diffing workaround, my own event delegation. The app is fast and small and easy to read. It is also mine in a way that a React app with 200 dependencies would not be. The tradeoff is real: I understand every line, which made debugging faster — and also meant there was nobody else's abstraction to blame when something broke.
